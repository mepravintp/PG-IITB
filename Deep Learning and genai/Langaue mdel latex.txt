\section*{Comparison of Language Modeling Approaches}

\renewcommand{\arraystretch}{1.5}
\setlength{\tabcolsep}{8pt}

\begin{tabular}{|>{\raggedright}m{2.5cm}|>{\raggedright}m{4cm}|>{\raggedright}m{3.5cm}|>{\raggedright}m{6cm}|>{\raggedright}m{3.5cm}|}
\hline
\textbf{Concept} & \textbf{Definition} & \textbf{Problem it Solves} & \textbf{How it Solves (Types, Formula, Example)} & \textbf{Limitation} \\
\hline

\textbf{N-gram LM} &
Statistical language model that predicts the next word based on the last $n$ words. &
Provides a simple way to estimate conditional probabilities of word sequences. &
Formula: $P(w_t|w_{t-n+1},...,w_{t-1})$. \newline
Example: Bigram model predicts ``sat'' after ``cat''. &
Suffers from sparsity (rare sequences), requires huge storage, cannot handle long-range dependencies. \\
\hline

\textbf{Perplexity} &
Evaluation metric for language models; measures how well a model predicts a sample. &
Quantifies predictive performance of a language model. &
Formula: $PP = 2^{-\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^N \log_2 P(w_i)}$. \newline
Example: Lower perplexity indicates better prediction accuracy. &
Only a numeric score; does not explain why errors occur or provide qualitative insight. \\
\hline

\textbf{RNN} &
Neural network with recurrent connections that processes sequences word by word. &
Handles variable-length input and captures sequential dependencies. &
Formula: $h_t = f(Wx_t + Uh_{t-1})$. \newline
Example: Predicting the next word in a sentence. &
Sequential processing (slow), vanishing/exploding gradients, struggles with long dependencies. \\
\hline

\textbf{LSTM} &
Advanced RNN with memory cell and gating mechanisms (input, forget, output gates). &
Addresses vanishing gradient problem and remembers long-term dependencies. &
Cell state update: $C_t = f_t \cdot C_{t-1} + i_t \cdot \tilde{C}_t$. \newline
Example: Captures relation between ``book'' and ``was amazing''. &
Computationally expensive, still sequential in nature. \\
\hline

\textbf{Seq2Seq} &
Encoder–Decoder RNN architecture mapping input sequence to output sequence of different length. &
Enables variable-length outputs, e.g., machine translation. &
Encoder compresses input into a vector; Decoder generates output step by step. \newline
Example: English sentence $\rightarrow$ French translation. &
Information bottleneck: entire input compressed into one vector, loses detail for long sentences. \\
\hline

\textbf{Attention} &
Mechanism that allows decoder to focus on relevant encoder states instead of a single vector. &
Solves Seq2Seq bottleneck, improves handling of long-range dependencies. &
Formula: $\text{Attention}(q,k,v) = \text{softmax}(qk^T)v$. \newline
Types: Additive (Bahdanau), Dot-product (Luong), Self-attention, Multi-head. \newline
Example: Decoder attends to ``yesterday'' when generating ``hier''. &
Adds complexity; self-attention has quadratic cost in memory and compute. \\
\hline

\end{tabular}
